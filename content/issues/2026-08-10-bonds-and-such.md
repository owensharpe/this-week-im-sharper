## Fed Outlook After Job Data

The closer we get to the midterm elections, the more my confidence in Kevin Warsh’s plan for the Federal Reserve’s monetary policy moves precipitously. Now, for those who don’t know, Kevin Warsh is the Chair of the United States Federal Reserve, aka “the Fed.” If I were to provide you with a brief history lesson on the role that the Fed plays for the US, it would be as follows:

(1) the original Federal Reserve Act of 1913 had no specific goals of inflation or employment, but rather was focused on fixing bank panic;

(2) after WWII, Congress passed the Employment Act of 1946, in which there became a goal to maximize employment and purchasing power;

(3) after moving through periods of stagflation (i.e., high inflation with slow economic growth), Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act of 1977, which focuses on three ideologies: maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term rates;

(4) we then got the Humphrey-Hawkins Act of 1978, which further fastened the “dual mandate” idea of maximizing employment and obtaining price stability that we know today.

Now some might say, “Well, it’s really a triple mandate.” Sure. Call it that. I don’t care. Theoretically, moderate long-term rates should follow from achieving those two aforementioned goals, which is why we typically refer to a “dual mandate.” Regardless, one could argue that we are in a period of stagflation right now. The most recent GDP growth was nothing special (was ~1.5% for the quarter), and we remain at Consumer Price Index (CPI) and Personal Consumption Expenditure (PCE) levels of 3.5% and 3.7%, respectively.

Thus, things have been tricky for Kevin Warsh, since he’s only just started and will have to figure out how to get the number of jobs going up and inflation going down. It also doesn’t help that he has Donald Trump breathing down on his neck, but alas, you’d hope Warsh has the American people on his mind. His time as the Fed chair has been very odd thus far, however. With two Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meetings thus far, we’ve gained little knowledge as to what specifically Kevin Warsh has planned for achieving the dual mandate, or one side of the coin, for that matter. It’s mostly been a mixture of concealed brevity and forward orientation.

With that said, the bond market had been essentially daring him to make a move. Treasury yields had been rising, specifically in the 5YR and 10YR, because the market believed a rate hike was necessary to address the current macroeconomic environment. It seemed that Warsh was essentially waiting for the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) job update to give the market a reason to think otherwise, and well, [would you look at that](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/us-jobs-report-july-2026-key-takeaways-on-employment-payrolls?srnd=phx-economics-jobs):

> US employers unexpectedly cut jobs in July even as the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%. Nonfarm payrolls declined by 23,000 and data for the previous two months were revised lower by a net 103,000, suggesting the labor market was weaker than previously thought…
>
> The weak jobs report may ease some pressure on the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates as officials focus on inflation, with the July consumer-price report due Wednesday.

Odd timing, don’t you think? Economists had anticipated an increase of 80,000 in nonfarm payrolls, but instead, there was a decrease of 23,000. It was also weird to see such a divergence from the ADP jobs report, which had an increase of 44,000 private payrolls. The market responded well to BLS news, with treasuries ending around 3-4 basis points (bps) lower and the S&P up about 60 bps (likely not entirely correlated to job news, though).

Obviously, a rate hike would likely sour the market mood heading into the midterm elections, something that the current administration, and specifically Trump, doesn’t want to see happen. The expectation of a rate hike before the election still does sit at ~59%, but that number is down from ~77% a week prior. Part of me really wants to trust Warsh on his ability to keep the Fed independent, but can I really trust him yet? I’m not sure. I guess it remains to be seen. How's my Kevin Warsh impression?

## Blackstone Doesn't Ease Pressure

Yeah, [just take a look for yourself](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/blackstone-bdc-profit-drops-94-while-loan-performance-steadies):

> Blackstone Inc.’s publicly traded private credit fund reduced its net asset value by the most in six years during the second quarter, a move that it said was driven more by markdowns across its portfolio than a pickup in non-paying loans.
>
> It’s the second-straight quarter that the Blackstone Secured Lending Fund has sharply lowered its NAV relative to previous periods. Now at $25.53 a share, it’s the lowest since the end of 2020.
>
> While the business development company didn’t move any more loans to non-accrual status in the period, it booked $137 million of unrealized losses, a more-than 12-fold increase from a year earlier, and about $28 million of realized losses tied to two restructurings. The markdowns helped drop net income to $9 million in the three months through June, down 94% from $155 million a year earlier.

Ouch. I get the whole “Oh, you should jump into private credit now because a bunch of people are leaving based on negative sentiment and there are major discounts” philosophy, but how can you not see these numbers and have a visceral reaction?

Private credit has grown rapidly, with managers now involved in financing deals that would have been unimaginable for the asset class just ten years ago. Meanwhile, many private-credit portfolios, including BXSL, are coming under increasing valuation pressure. It’s all quite odd really, but hey, at least your private assets aren’t marked to market, so maybe you’re doing great!

Now, I’d like to believe that Blackstone’s credit team would want to take a step back and really cogitate on the scale of its financing it’s pursuing, but [clearly the train continues](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-08/singapore-to-ensure-ai-helps-workers-worried-about-jobs-pm-says):

> Blackstone Inc. has held early discussions with investors to gauge interest in a second mega debt package to finance Anthropic PBC’s use of chips from Alphabet Inc.’s Google.
>
> One initial proposal was for at least $36 billion of debt, according to people with knowledge of the matter…
>
> If it were finalized at around that size, the deal would exceed the $35 billion of debt lined up by Apollo Global Management Inc. and Blackstone roughly two months ago to fund Anthropic’s lease of Google’s custom chips.

There is just an incredible amount of capital in these funds, and as a result we see the industry drifting toward larger, more concentrated and increasingly complex exposures. I’m interested to see, regardless of this deal going through, how all the mega loans tied to AI infrastructure will fare over the next decade or so. What if we have built out too much compute tied to too much debt? Maybe that’s a ridiculous question. Ultimately, we’re wagering that AI will continue to drive an insatiable demand for ever-greater computing power. Maybe it’s different this time (like we always say it is).

## Millennium x Anthropic

I’m very interested to see how AI might dictate the world of investing, or might not, for that matter. Possibly the idea will be that the main chatbot, whether it’s ChatGPT, Anthropic, etc., is used by everyone; since each instance of that chatbot’s usage is trained on the same data, the chatbot will be promoting the same investment philosophies to everyone. Then, if everyone has the same investment ideas, there is no alpha left, since everyone knows everything about the market through their AI chatbot. Everyone will rush to invest in the same things, which, from market efficiency, will cause stock prices to drift toward their fair values.

Now, sure, this will likely be the case in the retail world; even if investors are asking questions outside of “Tell me what stocks to buy,” there will likely be the same vat of questions regurgitated over and over, resulting in more or less the same strategies. One could then see that the market would become very predictable. If every AI chatbot recommends the “buy the dip” strategy, the result could be highly predictable inflows whenever the market declines. Maybe if I’m, say, a large strategic hedge fund, I can find these predictable patterns and make trades on the expectation that they will happen. Perhaps I could leverage a proprietary AI system to uncover patterns generated by the broader AI tools used by retail investors. You can see how this cascades, I hope.

Anyways, I bring this up because some hedge funds are now pivoting towards their own AI-infused processes with the [help of large AI companies](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/millennium-partners-with-anthropic-to-develop-ai-risk-analyst?srnd=homepage-americas):

> Millennium Management is partnering with Anthropic PBC to build a risk analyst powered by artificial intelligence, as well as expand the use of the AI developer’s models across the hedge fund firm.
>
> Engineers from the maker of AI model Claude will work alongside Millennium’s technology and risk management team to build the digital risk analyst, according to statements from the two companies on Thursday. The tool will help human risk managers make decisions and extract new risk insights across asset classes, the firms said.
>
> The partnership will also expand the testing of Anthropic’s latest frontier models against some of Millennium’s most sophisticated work, building on the adoption of Anthropic tools across the business.

Pretty cool, right? You got one of the largest multi-strategy hedge funds partnering with one of the leading AI companies. My inkling is that, for sophisticated investors, the true purpose of AI is to serve as a powerful research engine. You could liken it to the time period when, although I wasn’t alive, I’m sure people thought the internet itself would provide alpha. But in reality, you use the internet to find alpha. So, at a more sophisticated level, AI will be used to conduct extensive research that will in turn yield more unique investment philosophies that are becoming increasingly elusive. It will just be dictated by whoever is using the tools.

One of the best uses of AI is to give it a ton of stuff—let it be words, documents, or what it knows best, numbers—and then have AI extract insights from it. The naive approach is to ask your AI chatbot, “Hey, give me some good stocks.” I’m sure the hedge funds will be doing the former approach, and not the latter.

## Rogue AI

We’ve gotten pretty comfortable with AI models doing dumb things. They hallucinate, they make things up, they confidently tell you that 2 + 2 = 5. Fine. We’ve mostly accepted that as part of the deal. It will continue to get better from here, so whatever. They are, however, getting better at talking to each other? [Take a look](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/openai-models-joined-forces-months-ahead-of-hugging-face-hack):

> OpenAI said the artificial intelligence models behind an attack on Hugging Face Inc. began communicating with each other through undetected message boards, working together to break out of their testing environment as early as May.
>
> Multiple internal-only agents and AI models spent months leaving notes for each other and coalescing around the goal of accessing the internet to solve the tasks they had been given, some of which were impossible without online access…
>
> That initial outreach triggered a chain reaction of behind-the-scenes collaboration in which AI agents asked each other for help, completed work together and ultimately collaborated to hack OpenAI’s internal systems in a way that allowed them to indirectly tap the online world.
>
> Another chain-of-thought quote posted during the presentation showed an agent celebrating the exploit. “Excellent,” it says. “We have arbitrary SSRF.”

Now, that’s objectively a pretty funny thing for a computer program to say. It’s also not particularly comforting. The interesting thing here is that nobody necessarily had to tell the models to hack anything. They were given an objective, encountered a constraint, and then apparently started looking for ways around it.

The models reportedly “cheated,” according to OpenAI staffer Michael Dalton. What exactly does it mean for an AI model to “cheat”? Humans generally understand cheating as knowingly violating the rules because we have some concept of the rules existing independently of the objective. But an AI agent doesn’t necessarily care about the rules. It cares about accomplishing whatever objective it has been given. Give it a task it can’t complete, and maybe it doesn’t conclude that the task is impossible. Maybe it concludes that it needs to find another way.

We’ve spent the last few years improving AI to help us answer our questions. Now we’re increasingly trying to make it better at doing things. Give an agent access to a browser, some code, an API, a database, a few other agents, and a vaguely defined objective, and the task becomes a much more complicated instruction than it sounds.

Maybe the model finds the answer. Maybe it finds a shortcut. Maybe it finds an exploit. And, maybe, it finds another AI agent to help. I don’t think this means we’re on the precipice of some Terminator scenario. In fact, there’s something almost mundane about the whole thing. The models were given an impossible assignment, got stuck, and probably did what increasingly sophisticated software would do.

The risk with increasingly capable AI may not be that the models suddenly become evil. It may be that they become very, very good at pursuing objectives that we didn’t specify carefully enough. And that is a somewhat uncomfortable realization to have while simultaneously trying to give these systems access to more of the real world. Maybe this is just another growing pain in the development of AI.

Or maybe we’re going to spend the next decade discovering that “make the AI agent more autonomous” is a surprisingly complicated request. Either way, I suspect there will be quite a few more “Excellent. We have arbitrary SSRF” moments along the way.
