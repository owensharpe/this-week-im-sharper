## World of Warsh

Warsh and Co. have done it. The Fed has finally raised interest rates by 25 bps to combat inflation, bringing the federal funds rate to 3.75-4.00 bps. The vote was unanimous, 12-0. Here are [some other highlights](https://www.ft.com/content/f5ce5c38-76e3-4212-8c60-4c868f6dee70?syn-25a6b1a6=1#comments-anchor) that the FT touches upon:

> [The decision] comes as rate-setters attempt to stop sharp price rises triggered by the Middle East conflict and scramble for AI components from turning into a wider inflation crisis.
>
> “The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long,” Warsh said at a press conference following the two-day meeting. “This summer’s inflation readings do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved…”
>
> Short-term US government bond yields, which are sensitive to monetary policy expectations, rose following the Fed meeting. The two-year yield jumped 0.07 percentage points to 4.74 per cent, leaving it up almost half a percentage point since the central bank’s last meeting in July…
>
> Traders in the futures market priced in a nearly 90 per cent chance that the Fed will increase borrowing costs at least one more time before the end of this year…
>
> A dozen Fed officials projected an additional quarter-point rate rise by the end of 2026. Another four expected two rate rises, with two anticipating borrowing costs will remain at their current level.

I found the market's reaction pretty interesting. On Wednesday, major indexes fell, and yields rose further. Then yesterday, major indexes responded positively, and yields came down. Today, the market is pretty flat with yields heading back up. So what can we make of all this?

Well, I think there are a few reasons why markets reacted that way:

1. The bond market is getting some relief now. Yields rose considerably in the last month, primarily due to rising oil prices, competitive borrowing demand increasing bond supply, inter alia. This rate hike gave the market reassurance that the Fed will hopefully combat inflation into the future if the Iran War continues to be a detriment to the American consumer.
2. Coincidentally, Saudi Arabia began repairing its oil pipeline after it was damaged by two drone strikes, which prevented oil from being transported to the other coast. This effort caused oil prices to drop, thus pushing yields down a little bit.
3. There’s seemingly a global effort to bring yields down. Most of the other central banks (Bank of London, ECB, Bank of Japan, et cetera) either held or raised rates at their most recent meetings.

The bigger question, though, is what happens from here. The Fed can raise rates to cool demand, but it cannot produce more oil (duh!) or stop a war from disrupting supply (duh!). If oil prices continue to rise, the Fed may find itself fighting inflation that monetary policy cannot directly fix.

That is probably why the bond market's reaction has been so interesting. Investors are not just asking what the Fed did yesterday; they are asking what it will have to do next. If inflation continues to come in hot, another hike becomes more likely. If oil prices normalize and inflation begins to cool, the Fed may not need to do much more.

For now, the market seems to be stuck between those two possibilities (I believe). Yields remain elevated, and oil is still heavily dependent on what happens in the Middle East. The Fed has made its first move. Now we wait to see whether it actually works.

Which, as it turns out, is basically the entire job of the bond market: figuring out what the Fed is going to do before the Fed figures out what it is going to do. It never changes, does it?

## Private Credit Defaults Are Climbing

Once upon a time, there was a beautiful land of low interest rates. This was the post-COVID-19-shock landscape of mid 2020 into 2021, a major economic boom following the prior brief collapse of most economies in the first half of 2020. Companies could borrow at essentially 0%, and the economy was prosperous. Jerome Powell, the former chairman of the Fed, printed money as we’d never seen before, and everyone had lots of it. Companies could take on a lot of debt to expand their vigorous ambitions, and also hired many employees to meet surging demand for goods and services. It was an enviable world, and many investors miss it.

Most notably, I bet private credit investors miss it. As banks still had hefty regulatory restrictions regarding lending to riskier borrowers, megacap alternative investment firms (your Apollo’s, KKR’s, et cetera) stepped in and began winning a lot of middle-market and smaller company loan deals, particularly eating into BSL’s market share (when I say BSL, I mean the broadly syndicated loan market, which essentially means the loan (usually Term Loan Bs) deals facilitated through the banks to institutional investors, rather than investment firms directly lending to companies).

Private credit firms could win new deal flow by offering less restrictive covenants (agreements that require the borrower to do or avoid certain things), faster execution times, no exposure to public markets, et alia. Albeit, these companies typically pay higher rates on these loans as a result. These firms frequently lent to sponsor-backed companies, which typically meant those businesses took on higher levels of debt so the sponsor’s fund could target a higher equity internal rate of return. This strategy thrived in a low-rate environment: even with heavier debt loads, companies could easily cover interest expenses without financial strain.

This worked until it didn’t. An important thing to note with company loans is that they usually come with floating rates, which means that the makeup of the interest rate is typically SOFR plus a credit spread. You can define SOFR, or the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, as a benchmark interest rate based on the cost of borrowing cash overnight against U.S. Treasury securities in the repo market. Lenders often use the 1-month or 3-month Term SOFR rate when putting together the loan. This rate is added to a credit spread, which depends on idiosyncratic qualities pertaining to the borrowing company. SOFR replaced LIBOR, the London Interbank Offered Rate, this decade after a series of scandal issues that you can read about [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libor_scandal).

Anyways, the important thing to note is that when the federal funds rate soared in 2022 into 2023 over concerns about rampant inflation, the interest rates on those floating loans also soared. It hasn’t helped recently that the darling of those direct lending firms was middle-market enterprise software companies, most of whom have widening exposure to AI development.

Then, what do you end up with? You end up with a bunch of debt-ridden companies that are paying higher interest rates than they were a few years ago, and couple that with the idea that a lot of them have business models directly exposed to the largest and most blossoming technology of all time. Yeah, it’s not great. That’s why you saw a lot of funds at these alternative investment firms this year have shares trading well below net asset value (NAV).

Naturally, we run into situations [like this](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/us-private-credit-default-rate-hits-a-record-of-6-3-fitch-says):

> The trailing 12-month private credit default rate across 1,300 borrowers rose to 6.3% at the end of last month, surpassing the previous high of 6.1% in the prior month, said a report released Monday. Fitch also recorded the highest monthly total of private credit default events in the past year.
>
> “Private credit defaults continued to rise in August, driven by maturity extension transactions that Fitch deems defaults, as uncertainty around rates and inflation has stifled deal flow, making it difficult for sponsors to sell struggling portfolio companies ahead of loan maturities,” said Lyle Margolis, Fitch’s head of private credit for North America.
>
> There were 14 recorded instances, out of which 11 involved unique defaulters, while three involved serial defaulters, according to the report. Interest payment deferrals and the introduction of payment-in-kind in lieu of cash interest accounted for 47% of the 89 defaults that occurred over the past year…
>
> Looking at industries, the highest default activity was registered by healthcare, industrial and manufacturing. The sectors each recorded a 9.9% default rate last month, up from 9.5% in July.
>
> Despite a software sector slowdown stymied by investor fears tied to artificial intelligence disruption this year, the industry “continued to have the lowest default rate” among those rated by Fitch. The technology software sector posted a default rate of 0.6%, down from 1.2% the prior month.

A few things I want to point out. The majority of private credit defaults recently have been maturity extensions that Fitch is deeming as defaults. Essentially, a borrower may be counted as having defaulted even when lenders agree to modify or extend the loan rather than immediately pursue repayment. In this case, we had instances where borrowers were given grace periods to pause interest payments or were allowed to pay the interest using additional debt or equity (that’s what Payment-In-Kind, or PIK, is). Sketchy, not credit-investor-pleasing stuff.

Looking at those default rate numbers for healthcare, industrial, and manufacturing, that is pretty rough. A 10% default rate is very high, implying stress in that sector; about 1 in every 10 loans is suffering a default. Not good! Lastly, it’s funny I mentioned that software companies were suffering the most because of AI, and yet that sector actually has the lowest default rate among all sectors. Funny how this stuff works, right?

Anywho, [we’ve seen recently](https://octus.com/resources/blog/ooc-private-credit-debt-flocks-to-bsl-market-for-refinancing/) that these two areas, private credit and the BSL market, [are actually refinancing eachother’s debt](https://octus.com/resources/articles/strong-levfin-conditions-attract-issuers-to-tap-bsl-market-to-refinance-private-credit-debt/?utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_campaign=octus-on-credit). Isn’t that lovely? It’s all just one big incestuous mix of debt, and it’s frothing at the wrong time.

## AI Will Make Our Drugs Now

I see there being two sides to the AI in Pharma debate:

1. AI is the greatest thing that humanity has ever created. AI will push the frontier and create new boundaries for what is plausible in the pharma space. We will be able to create drugs that can cure any disease, any illness, and people will live forever. God bless the human race.
2. AI will kill us all, and we hate AI. AI is going to build powerful viruses/bioweapons that can take out specific populations or humanity as a whole. It would be stupid to allow the creation of an open-source model, let alone a model based on the partnership of a pharma company with an AI lab, with capabilities of possibly ending the world. What happens when the bad guy wins?

Maybe the first guy is that VC bro who doesn’t shut up about what he’s invested in, and the second guy is some, I don’t know, [effective altruists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism)? Regardless, AI is scary. It seems to supposedly have unbounded potential to be really, really awesome or really, really awful. Maybe it will be somewhere in the middle? That will be my bet. Anyways, I bring this up because we’re [getting a new partnership](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/novo-will-work-with-anthropic-ai-to-speed-drug-development):

> Novo Nordisk A/S and Anthropic PBC will work together to speed up drug development using artificial intelligence, a key goal as Novo tries to catch up in the cutthroat obesity market…
>
> Initially, Novo’s R&D department will use Anthropic’s Claude Science to tackle scientific problems identified by researchers where the companies’ joint capabilities are seen as having the greatest impact. The goal is to compress the path from research to marketed product, and ultimately to use AI to find “completely new scientific opportunities,” according to Doustdar…
>
> Earlier this month, Anthropic revealed that people had used Claude in ways that could support the development of biological weapons, though it said the cases were ambiguous and the research could have been benign. Novo said its collaboration had been designed with “robust data governance and human oversight, helping ensure AI is applied responsibly.”

Boom. There’s the key right there: “helping ensure AI is applied responsibly.” But how can we know that? I mean, this is the same company that has researchers earnestly bragging about the fact that [their technology has a 10% chance of killing people](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/09/anthropic-researcher-quits-ai-safety.html)! And now, we get to see them partner up with a company that makes drugs for the benefit of…money! Less so for people, but you get the point.

Also, nobody has demonstrated that Claude can reliably invent a blockbuster drug. Novo and Anthropic are starting by testing the technology on specific research problems, with human oversight and data-governance controls. But that is probably the more interesting way to think about the AI boom anyway. The question, in my eyes, will be whether a scientist with an AI model can do in six months what previously took a year.

Everything is progressing so fast, though. To think we’re only 4 years removed from a world without public AI is quite mind-boggling. AI in drug-discovery was the supposed sci-fi horror story spoken of for a long time, and now, while incredibly nascent, it has begun.

## Things Get Larger and More Volatile

Things Get Larger and More Volatile

I have a few takes in my arsenal. Some takes I will never share because I think they’re stupid; I’ll most likely be a coward about them and possibly reveal them if I’m right, and bury them away if I’m wrong. I would bet most people do that too. There is a different take, though, that I will always harp on; I would also bet that many people agree with this one.

The world is naturally becoming more volatile. What do I mean by this? I can apply this expression to many different things. [Human sentiment has naturally become more volatile] (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12545191/): take a look at any social or economic issue, and you’ll find much more disagreement and polarizing views than you did half a century, or even a decade ago. The impetus for this is probably the rise of social media and echo chambers, but regardless, it persists.

People react to events much more quickly and expressively. However, people move on from these events as soon as possible to latch onto a fresh new topic rather than attempt to reason with the topic and perform critical thinking. [This is apparent on social media as well](https://www.inc.com/jessica-stillman/feel-like-time-is-speeding-up-scientists-have-figured-out-why-and-what-to-do-about-it/91404820): we see major events like assassinations happen, and people talk about it for a few days and move on to the next new thing. I’ve done my best to mitigate this issue from seeping into my life, but it can be difficult at times.

Then there is a much more glaring one: markets have become much more volatile. When I say markets, I mean the idiosyncratic volatility among single stocks rather than indexes as a whole, and [this paper hardens my point](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7590).

All of this implies that, yes, we are seeing heightened volatility and negative sentiment towards everything now. As a result, we should get used to the times. For one, it seems that yields may remain elevated for an extended period, given that the current administration has little interest in solving our debt crisis. Our interest now sits at over $1 trillion USD, and that won’t come down anytime soon; our debt as a percentage of GDP has also continued to climb over the years. This is the new normal.

In highly volatile environments, bid-ask spreads will widen, since traders face fast-moving prices and high hedging costs when attempting to close positions; we get almost a deluge or waterfall in terms of the price chart when this movement occurs. Who serves to win the most from the spreads widening, you might ask?

Well, it would be the market makers, of course, because they make money off the spreads of these order books. If I could suggest to someone what they should do if they want to make a lot of money, go work at a market maker. I mean, there’s only going to be more people trading over time. It goes like this:

1. Naturally (also hopefully, assuming society can make sure birth rates are at about 2% or more), there will be more people;
2. These people love social media, and many are addicted to it, so they will go on it a lot;
3. These people will receive a bunch of short-form content targeted towards proclaiming they can make money quickly through the stock market (or some other market, maybe prediction markets or something);
4. They will go onto their brokerage app with very little knowledge and execute a bunch of trades, sometimes leveraging up to do so as well;
5. The market makers will appreciate this heightened volume and will reap the rewards should the market have more volatilty, particularly in large drawdowns.
6. Revenue from these market makers will be huge and continue to scale as firms receive even more order flow from retail.

I’m no prophet, but let’s see if I’m right.
