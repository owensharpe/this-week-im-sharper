## More Data Centers

A gigawatt is a lot of energy. I don’t know too much about energy units, so whenever there’s a new data center headline about “X company will need Y amount of energy for its new $Z data center,” I can never discern what the scale really is. I know a gigawatt is 1,000,000,000 watts, but like, how large is that relative to other things? I thought it might be helpful to list some associated things with units of energy to give you a better sense of how large a gigawatt is:

- 1 W: Small LED indicator
- 5 W: Phone charging
- 100 W: Bright incandescent bulb
- 300 W: Light Gaming PC
- 1,000 W: Microwave
- 5,000 W: EV charging at a high rate
- 10,000 W: Large home electrical load
- 100,000 W: Small commercial building
- 1,000,000 W: Large industrial facility
- 10,000,000 W: Large data center
- 100,000,000 W: Very large data center
- 1,000,000,000 W: Data center campus (1 Gigawatt)

Pretty interesting, in my humble opinion. You can see how rapidly the scale increases, turning into massive projects as soon as we cross the 1 million-watt threshold. And these large projects also cost a lot, believe it or not. [Check out this new one that Nvidia is doing with OpenAI](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/nvidia-to-invest-up-to-105-billion-for-openai-data-center-in-ohio):

> Nvidia Corp. has agreed to spend as much as $105 billion to support a massive new data center campus in Ohio set to be leased by OpenAI, according to a financial filing, marking the latest tie-up between two dominant forces driving the AI boom.
>
> The ChatGPT maker said Monday it has entered into an agreement to secure up to roughly 8 gigawatts of computing capacity from the Pike County complex, with the first 800 megawatts expected to come online by 2028. A single gigawatt is enough to power up to 750,000 US homes at any given time.

8 freaking gigawatts. $105 billion! It feels like no one is blinking an eye at these numbers anymore because they’ve become more and more ubiquitous, but my goodness. This one will be based in Ohio, underscoring how the Midwest and the South have become prime real estate for companies racing to secure land for massive data centers. I wonder how the Pike County residents feel about this? I’ve never even seen a data center in person before, or at least one of these mammoths. Can’t be a nice feeling to live next to one of these things.

What’s also very interesting to me is the drop-offs in guarantee amounts speculated in these deals. [Going back a few weeks earlier when the deal was being discussed](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/nvidia-in-talks-on-250-billion-backing-for-openai-hub-wsj-says), we can see:

> Nvidia is in discussions to help the creator of ChatGPT lease a $500 billion, 10-gigawatt hub that SoftBank Group Corp. is overseeing in Ohio, people familiar with the matter said. The US chipmaker may provide a guarantee of as much as $250 billion to the AI lab, one of the people said.

I’m wondering if this is due to Nvidia’s overall perception of the AI market, or just its sentiment toward OpenAI. If I recall, this has happened a few times now regarding companies stepping back from their large funding promises to OpenAI. It doesn’t help that as of yesterday, OpenAI’s revenue grew 18% QoQ whereas Anthropic more than doubled its revenue QoQ.

Regardless, people now argue that OpenAI’s recent AI model is better than Anthropic’s, and Codex has seemingly gained a lot of ground in the last month or so. I’m of the opinion that we really won’t be able to tell what’s happening until both these companies are public and must report more frequently. The most important thing is that these models are becoming commoditized, and may the cheapest one win. Let’s see how China comes into the frame.

## Private Credit Does More Weird Stuff

One of my goals for this newsletter is to go a period of time without writing about private credit. However, whenever I open Bloomberg or the FT, I get hit with a fascinating new article about how ominous and bizarre the private credit space is. The oodles of weird financial engineering are just too good for me not to write about them.

However, I do think some of the hysteria is somewhat overblown. If you don’t mind, I’m going to go through a handful of statistical notes I’ve found amongst an FT article, and let you know how I feel.

Read the [FT article](https://www.ft.com/content/67acde0d-4154-4332-b33b-2d03d3a86007?syn-25a6b1a6=1#comments-anchor) if you’d like:

> Loans placed on non-accrual status by the 20 largest publicly traded business development companies (BDCs) — listed funds that invest in private credit loans — climbed to a median 2.8 per cent of their cost in the second quarter, up from 2 per cent at the end of March.

A loan is placed on non-accrual status when the borrower has started to miss payments, or when the fund believes the borrower is unlikely to repay the loan. Again, I get we are seeing MoM increases in non-accrual loans, but those numbers intrinsically are still very small. I could tell you that the number of non-accrual status loans from the largest BDCs is up roughly 40% since March, but without the underlying numbers, I can paint a picture that seems worse than it actually is.

However, it is important to note that it’s not a good direction, as obvious as that may be. We just need to wait a little longer to see whether the rate of change increases or decreases to get more sentiment.

A statistic that is a little more concerning is this one:

> [FS KKR Capital Group] reported that 7.1 per cent of its loan book was troubled in the second quarter, a slight improvement from the prior quarter but still far above the industry average.

Saying “troubled” is definitely a safer description than “non-accrual,” but that figure is very high. In my mind, that’s suggesting there may be a much larger pool of loans that haven't technically defaulted yet but are deteriorating. We also don’t know the specifics of how KKR classifies “troubled” loans. For example, KKR’s definition of “troubled” could differ significantly from that of Blue Owl; we just don’t know.

Then I move to some quotes from senior employees at Ares and Blue Owl, who say:

> Craig Packer, Blue Owl’s co-president, told investors in one of the firm’s funds that “credit metrics are healthy and the issues we are managing remain isolated.”
>
> Jim Miller, who runs Ares’ US direct lending business, said borrowers were in “solid” shape, noting that “interest coverage and leverage levels were generally consistent with our five-year average.”

I feel that credit quality is becoming increasingly idiosyncratic. Some managers, like the ones above, are saying their borrowers are perfectly healthy. Other funds are seeing meaningful portions of their books become troubled. Meanwhile, the industry-wide non-accrual rate is rising. I think this shows that the underwriting strategies employed by firms were not ubiquitous.

And then there’s also [this article](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/jefferies-targets-1-billion-for-private-credit-secondaries-fund) that came out as I was writing my main piece:

> Jefferies Credit Partners is seeking to raise around €1 billion ($1.16 billion) from investors who trade private credit in the secondary market.
>
> The fund will acquire loans from its existing portfolio and provide fresh capital for new lending, according to people familiar with the matter…
>
> As a continuation vehicle, or staple deal, the Jefferies Credit Partners fund will comprise about €500 million of equity and €500 million of leverage, the people said, asking not to be identified because the information is private. Jefferies advised on the transaction, the people said.
>
> The evergreen fund will be able to recycle capital indefinitely. It will hold loans previously originated by Jefferies Credit Partners.

Since I’ve started reading the news, I don’t even think I’ve seen this before. The same firm is the buyer, seller, and advisor? So Jeffries is advising a deal in which Jeffries Credit Partners is selling loans to…Jeffries Credit Partners? I guess. It’s technically a secondaries deal, but I would love to see the reasoning for Jeffries to do this.

It seems that, to me, there are a few reasons:

1. Jeffries is trying to move loans out of an old finite fund into an “evergreen fund”, which is just a fancy term for an investment vehicle with no termination date (unlike the finite fund). With this new evergreen fund, it can just continuously deploy capital should its borrowers repay the loans.
2. The investors in the old fund get liquidity while Jeffries retains the loans. Good for the old investors to get out, and good for Jeffries to continue to manage the same loans.
3. Jeffries would get about €500mm in equity investment from new investors to turn into €1bn in assets. That’s more of an opportunity to amplify returns.

Although if I’m an investor here, I’d still be skeptical about why Jeffries wants to transfer these loans in particular. I’m sure it’s all fine!

## Prediction Markets Take A Step Further

[Lol](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/cantor-lets-hedge-funds-place-big-bets-on-kalshis-prediction-markets-625d21e7?mod=finance_lead_pos1):

> Cantor Fitzgerald plans to give investment funds access to Kalshi’s prediction markets.
>
> Cantor’s some 3,000 institutional clients, which range from family offices to hedge funds, will have full access to Kalshi’s suite of events-based, yes-or-no contracts such as those tied to weather, commodities and corporate results...
>
> Cantor plans to serve as a broker to those clients, buying or selling blocks of event contracts and often helping to distribute those holdings to other investors through private negotiations.
>
> [Pascal] Bandelier said that hedge funds have expressed interest in trading event contracts tied to iPhone sales rather than trying to anticipate how Apple shares will react to sales numbers. Family offices have interest in hedging risks related to weather or commodities such as crop outputs and oil prices, he added.

This is funny. Reading the progression of prediction markets over the last year has been quite the ride. Now we are in a world where instituional investors (call it hedge funds, family offices, pension funds, etc.) can gamble their money! I'm sure there will be some amount of insider trading involved as well. We'll have to wait and see.

I see no mention that Cantor is brokering exclusively institution-to-institution trades, so presumably retail investors could eventually be on the other side of some of these positions. And if history is any guide, eventually someone will find a way to package these trades for retail. If you have no edge in prediction markets, don't play the game!

Cantor almost feels like a bookie in this instance, right? Or really, maybe they're a bookie-like intermediary for institutional prediction-market bets. Only a matter of time until we get event contracts in 401k's. Bring on the sports gambling ETFs!
