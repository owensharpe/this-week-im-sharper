## Systematic Credit Investing

Over the past few years, I've tried to really discern what I want to do when I’m older. I would say that the list of “What does Owen Sharpe like doing and learning about?” is probably a myriad of weird, distinct things. There are things I’d say I’m MORE interested in, though. Let’s briefly walk through some of those:

1. I really like math;
2. I really like to read the news and learn about markets;
3. I really like data;
4. I really like using coding as a tool for research;
5. I really like downside risk assessment, but that really just means that I’m very cynical and skeptical of things.

The list goes on, and maybe you see where I’m going with this point. Please understand that I’m interested in identifying an intersection in these states, sets, et cetera. My “extensive” (sort of only played around with observing cross-sections of this space, hence the quotes) research has led me to grow an affinity for systematic credit investing.

Now, one could ask the totally reasonable question: what is systematic credit investing? This term is blending a lot of things, so let’s make it easy to understand. Okay, well, investing is hopefully straightforward; investing [means] (https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/investing) “to commit (money) in order to earn a financial return.” We put money in to get more money out. Now what is credit? We’d think of credit broadly as an extended IOU, or [more formally] (https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/credit), “the provision of money, goods, or services with the expectation of future payment.” Credit can be a loan, bond, et cetera. An entity, let it be a person, company, or government, can go and get credit from someone else to create financing. This can prove lucrative for the company if, let’s say, the interest rate on that credit is a good deal (that’s a whole different story).

Then there is systematic. I’ll give you the formal definition, [which is] (https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/systematic): “methodical in procedure or plan.” Maybe you’re still confused, though, which I’d totally understand. In the quantitative finance realm, we’d think of a systematic strategy as one that draws on algorithms or signals designed to catch patterns in (increasingly alternative) data, rather than your typical investor looking at qualitative and quantitative facts and fundamentally choosing a winner.

So when we combine all these things, systematic credit investing means using various algorithms or signals to identify patterns within credit data to garner a return on the initial money you put down. Makes sense? You specialise in building models that help predict credit features or draw insights from vast amounts of data. We allow machines to pick winners for us, rather than picking winners ourselves, if you will.

I would say my major asset-class interests don’t pertain to just credit; fixed-income is a very broad space, and I’ve read different articles saying credit is enveloped within fixed-income and vice versa. I think the consensus is that credit is a subset of fixed-income. Rates, government debt, and agency bonds are interesting in their own right.

Anyways, another thing that is super interesting about credit/fixed-income is the lack of transparent data. This is still a very opaque market. There are thousands of securities or products in this space, and the amount of outstanding securities dwarfs that of the equity market. You can think of a company like Apple, for example. Think of Apple stock as just stock, where you can go on an exchange (by way of your broker, probably), buy a share of Apple based on the order book, and then boom, I own a share of Apple.

Now this should largely make sense, as there is one Apple ticker, AAPL, and I know what I’m getting when I buy a share. Conversely, Apple has dozens of bonds outstanding. Each Apple bond has its respective coupon, maturity date, varying amount of identifiers, et cetera. I can’t just go onto an exchange and get “the Apple bond.” There are too many to choose from.

It also doesn’t help that corporate bonds generally trade Over-The-Counter (OTC) rather than through a centralised exchange/order book. There is no centralised area for people to trade bonds. The middleman in this scenario, unlike the exchange in the prior scenario, is likely a dealer or bank. You often had to call up the dealer/bank and ask for a quote. I might have to call up Johnny at X Wall Street Bank and say, “Hey Johnny, I want to buy Y of Z specific bond. Can you do that for me?” Then Johnny might say, “Well, I got this ask price of A; I can sell it to you at that,” and maybe that is from the bank’s inventory, or coming from an investor who’s trying to sell (excuse my poor Wall Street colloquialisms).

Needless to say, we call this an OTC trade. That term historically comes from people physically doing deals like this over the counter. Nowadays, you would say that a deal is OTC if it’s not coming from a centralised exchange. Exchanges offer the benefit of very liquid stocks that essentially show real-time prices. These fixed-income securities and institutional loans are still relatively illiquid compared to their equity counterparts. Some resources exist for observing historical bond prices, like TRACE (Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine), which disseminates transaction information for eligible fixed-income securities. LSTA (Loan Syndications and Trading Association) provides market data and infrastructure for leveraged loans. These resources help drive down the opacity of these markets.

The more liquid fixed-income spaces are getting more electronic, though, and the view in my eyes is that will only continue as data becomes more widely available and technology advances. The main distinction is that there isn't a single centralised, continuously updated order book covering the entire bond/loan market like you have for a stock such as AAPL. That’s why I’d like to be on the forefront of this growth. Pretty cool, no?

I bring all this up because there are some great introductory white papers if you’re interested. For example, this [AQR] (https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Insights/White-Papers/Systematic-Credit-Investing.pdf) one is a good start. I’m going to build out a few strategies in my free time and then get back to you on what I find. The annoying part is gathering data, based on what I’ve said prior. This is the most interesting space for growth for asset classes, though, in my humble opinion.

## Nvidia Does Everything Now

It’s pretty remarkable what has happened to Nvidia this decade. For me, I only knew of Nvidia before 2020 for its GeForce gaming services. Since the turn of the decade, it's up about 3,600%. Not too shabby, Nvidia, not too shabby. Ever since 2023, it’s become the bellwether of the AI boom. As a result, it garners strong correlations with whatever news comes out, whether that’s good or bad, and often sets the precedent for other major AI players.

However, I’d say this year in particular has led it to provide very interesting headlines. We can point to the circular financing predicament, among other things, but a few new headlines came out that were pretty fun. How about I provide my two cents on a few of them?

The [first of which] (https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-28/nvidia-backed-lambda-inks-1-billion-private-debt-for-chip-deal) was directly focused on Nvidia-backed Iren, a neocloud company:

> Lambda Inc., an AI cloud-computing provider backed by Nvidia Corp., has raised about $1 billion of private short-dated debt for computing chips tied to its collaboration with Microsoft Corp...
>
> The debt will finance the purchase of Nvidia graphics processing units known as GPUs, which will be leased by Microsoft, they added.

The concern, raised previously, is that Nvidia is supporting companies through equity investments, enabling them to incur debt that is then used to buy Nvidia products. This approach is analogous—though not identical—to Nvidia’s revenue-sharing model, in which the company offers credit support to AI cloud firms in exchange for a share of their revenues.

Now, Nvidia recently paused its revenue-sharing model a week or two ago, but these types of debt deals feel somewhat similar, just with the equity investment and then debt take-on replacing the outright credit support with revenue sharing. Hopefully you can see the comparison I’m drawing there? It appears Nvidia is engaging in a comparable practice, but now with less public attention. Meanwhile, debt issuance among tech companies shows no signs of slowing down. Adding to that, the GPUs will be leased by the hyperscaler Microsoft, so that means the private placement probably had a lower coupon because it’s considered less risky. Yay, I guess.

Well, guess what: Iren wasn’t done, as [another debt deal got placed] (https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-28/blue-owl-leads-2-4-billion-iren-debt-deal-to-buy-nvidia-s-chips), and it was for more Nvidia products:

> Blue Owl Capital Inc. funds led a $2.4 billion debt deal for cloud computing provider Iren Ltd., which will use the proceeds to buy Nvidia Corp.’s Blackwell Ultra GPUs for a data center campus in Canada…
>
> The debt will help support the build-out of computing capacity for Iren’s Mackenzie data center campus in British Columbia.

So, more of what I just mentioned above. Nice Nvidia, nice. They also [just went out and bought Hugging Face] (https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/nvidia-agrees-to-13-billion-deal-for-ai-platform-hugging-face), the company I’d define as an open-source model community website:

> Nvidia Corp. agreed to acquire artificial intelligence startup Hugging Face in a transaction valued at about $13 billion, adding a popular software platform to the chip giant’s AI empire…
>
> Nvidia touted the deal as a way to protect open-source AI developers, who rely on Hugging Face to share software and data that can be freely used by anyone…
>
> OpenAI, Anthropic PBC and other big AI developers mostly rely on closed models that are proprietary. In buying Hugging Face, Nvidia vowed to keep the platform open.

Hugging Face is pretty awesome, and its transformers library is considered one of the best for deep learning training, but $13B? That might’ve been a little steep. Interestingly, the reported price was $12,930,300,000.00. If you take the first six digits, 129303, and convert that to a Unicode code point, you get U+1F917 (the 🤗 emoji)! That’s pretty funny and an awesome little easter egg. It only costs hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars, I’m sure, though.

Regardless, I do see the benefit here for Nvidia. As Nvidia, you just want people using AI, since most of the infrastructure AI sits upon is built partly or entirely by Nvidia. You want to be model-agnostic, which simply means it wouldn’t matter whether those models are open-source or closed. Going and purchasing Hugging Face lets you tap into that open-source realm that Nvidia might have been lacking previously. I’m just sure the $13B asking price may have been a bit much for what Hugging Face offers.

## Astra

We just got Astra this week; OpenAI is seemingly back into the lead in the ever-changing AI race with GPT-6. It seemed like Anthropic had the lead for a good year and a half, but now, ahead of its IPO, OpenAI may have stifled some of the excitement.

Everyone is going to talk about the benchmarks, which I understand, but I think we’re seeing they’re moot points since any AI company can tailor specific benchmarks to its benefit, and every new frontier model will just beat the other one out. Also, the average human neither cares nor understands the benchmarks the AI companies provide.

The more important view, in my mind, is how you market the model towards user needs, and also at a low price. The consumer just cares about “How can I get the model to do what I want and at the cheapest price possible?” Some open models—especially those from China—can offer token input and output pricing at significantly lower costs. This highlights the challenge facing Anthropic and OpenAI, who may be charging too much for their services. However, their high prices are partly driven by the cash-intensive nature of their businesses; eventually, both companies must achieve profitability.

Another major issue is security. OpenAI asserts Astra is its first model to feature advanced cybersecurity capabilities, potentially putting it on par with Anthropic’s Mythos. Now, is this possibly hype? To a certain extent, yes. We’ve seen AI companies do this before; the fear-mongering strategy to increase valuation is nothing new. There is still concern with these cyber issues, though.

My concern with current cyber infrastructure is that it is relatively static. These new AI agents are able to move dynamically, and by that I mean that they are able to edit and improve code in real time. When tested against these static systems, the agents can figure out how to eventually get around them by trying every trick in the book. The hope is that frontier AI companies will give their best models to IT companies to help create dynamic systems that can combat the dynamic agents. I’m essentially spitballing on this, but here’s a [good quote] (https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/01/open-ai-astra-cyber-model.html):

> The company decided to delay parts of Astra’s development even though the model was not involved in the Hugging Face incident. After strengthening and testing protections, OpenAI said Tuesday that it believes the model’s safeguards “sufficiently minimize the risk of severe harm for release under our Preparedness Framework.”

Yeah, let’s see where this takes us.

## Tesla Unveils The Cybercab

Here comes Tesla! It just unveiled its new Cybercab to supposedly compete with Google’s Waymo and Amazon’s Zoox. So I didn’t even get to see videos from this event; I only got my information from various news sources.

Good news on the video front. There weren't any! No livestream, no Musk, just an invite-only event in Austin with five sweepstakes winners and reportedly some NDAs. You probably saw exactly as much as everyone else. What happened is Tesla started charging for rides in the Cybercab, its two-seater with no steering wheel and no pedals. About 45 are registered in Texas. Waymo is doing 500,000 paid rides a week across 14 cities. Zoox has been charging money on the Las Vegas Strip since August. Tesla is third, and it is not close.

Then the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) opened an investigation into whether Tesla was allowed to sell the thing at all. In the US, you certify your own compliance with federal safety standards, and the regulator comes after you later. Tesla's position is that the rule about steering wheels does not apply to a car without one. The alternative was to request a formal exemption, but that caps you at 2,500 vehicles a year, and 2,500 is an insult if your stock thesis is a million robotaxis. Zoox tried the same move in 2022, got hit, recalled the fleet, and spent four years going the long way around.

That brings us to the money. Tesla trades near 200 times earnings on a business whose revenue shrank last year. A large chunk of Tesla is a product a federal agency spent last Thursday looking into. Musk's trillion-dollar pay package includes a milestone of one million robotaxis in operation. He is at 45!

Tesla’s stock fell 6% Friday. With such a wide price target range, traditional business valuation has given way to a referendum on faith in Musk himself.
