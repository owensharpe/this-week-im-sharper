## Collateralized Fund Obligations

I love it when I learn about new financial instruments because they always get weirder and more obscure, but ironically share bits and pieces with other instruments from the past that have gone out of style. I came across [this article](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/pimco-s-stracke-says-some-complicated-debt-echoes-subprime-mbs?srnd=undefined) while reading one morning, and the term “collateralized fund obligation” caught my eye. I don’t think I’d ever seen this type of collateralized debt obligation (CDO) before, so I had to search the internet for guidance. I came to find out, I guess, that this is a massively growing instrument and one of the most popular right now. According to the aforementioned article,

> Collateralized fund obligations are one of the fastest-growing instruments in the over-$1 trillion market known as fund finance, where money managers raise cash by transforming shares of funds — a form of equity — into credit. In related products, banks can use significant risk transfers to shift loan exposure to third parties…
> Because insurers are typically required to mostly buy high-quality debt, these instruments are frequently designed to carry high ratings from bond graders.

And from Christian Stracke, the president of Pimco: “they’re examples of complacency, they’re examples of people under-pricing the risk to get leverage.” So this instrument is really just a specific flavor of CDO, and I can explain what that is. A CDO is a structured investment vehicle that pools together debt or other assets and then divides the cash flows from those assets into different layers, called tranches, each with a different level of risk and return.

Typically, you might have an AAA tranche at the top, followed by lower-rated tranches such as AA, BBB, and BB, and then an equity tranche at the bottom. The tranches at the top are the safest because they get paid first and are protected from losses until the lower tranches have absorbed them. The equity tranche is the riskiest, meaning that it takes losses first, but in exchange, it has the potential for the highest return.

There are various types of CDOs depending on what is being pooled together, like collateralized loan obligations (CLOs), collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs), collateralized bond obligations (CBOs), and I guess there’s a new example: a collateralized fund obligation (CFO). You can even have CDO-squared, where the underlying assets are themselves tranches of other CDOs (isn’t that great?).

So what is a CFO? Well, I just told you what a CDO is; maybe you can piece this one together. In a CFO, each tranche contains different investment funds, a securitization of different fund interests, if you will. We still have that same tranche structure where different tranches provide varying levels of risk and return; in the case of a CFO, each tranche can have exposures to funds, potentially private equity funds, hedge funds, real estate funds, private credit funds, et cetera. It’s really a matter of whatever tickles your fancy.

The distinction with CFOs is that the underlying assets, or pool of collateral, is much harder to value and much less liquid than, say, the underlying assets of a CLO. This makes sense given the exposure can be to private market funds, where things are not marked to market, and there is no secondary market that offers insight into the financial health of the companies inside the funds. That is why Stracke is raising this concern.

I’ll also implore you to read this [good source](https://www.dechert.com/knowledge/publication/2025/1/dechert-s-global-finance-team-publishes-chapter-on-collateralize.html) for more about CFOs if you’d like, because I think they’re interesting instruments that will continue to gain traction as investors flock to more exposure to various funds, especially in retail.

## Significant Risk Transfers

Another thing I saw that I didn’t know before was significant risk transfers. This also came up in [that article about Pimco](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/pimco-s-stracke-says-some-complicated-debt-echoes-subprime-mbs?srnd=undefined), so I thought I’d write about it too. We can think about an SRT simply as a bank transferring portions of credit risk to other investors without having to sell the underlying loans entirely. SRTs share some similarities with the CDOs mentioned earlier; however, while CDOs mostly bundle assets, SRTs focus on transferring portions of credit risk. This allows banks to lessen their capital requirements without selling the underlying loans.

From what I’ve read, SRTs can hold cash, but also synthetic securitizations (this is the primary use case); investors can get exposure through the various senior, mezzanine, and junior tranches. The way it works, from the bank’s POV, is as follows: I’m the bank, and I really like the borrowers that are on the loans I’ve given, but I’m concerned about default risk; I need someone else to absorb a portion of the credit risk of these loans should the borrower default; I then find investors who are willing to take on that risk, and I’ll pay them for doing so; I, the bank, would do this such that I don’t have to hold as much regulatory capital for the risk that I would be taking on, should I have the whole loan. Thus, I can use my capital in other places. That’s basically the reason for an SRT.

It’s interesting that European SRT issuance dwarfs that of the issuance from the US. Clearly, there is a lot of room for this vehicle to grow here domestically, so I wouldn’t be surprised if that was the case. In the US, SRTs have been used to help with freeing up capital and liquidity, whereas in Europe, the focus is towards optimziing that capital amidst tighter capital constraints.

An [important note](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/insurers-want-a-bigger-slice-of-the-bank-risk-transfer-boom) from a few weeks ago highlighted that insurers are increasingly trying to get into these new SRT deals since they offer diversification and greater yields relative to other fixed-income instruments. I'd like to point out this quote as an ominous reminder:

> The Bank of England is concerned that this type of deal may leave banks exposed to greater risk at times of stress because it relies on insurers being able to honor their guarantees.

Banks do love getting risk on after too long, don't they? Once again, I have a [great resource](https://www.kkr.com/insights/asset-based-finance-srt) that goes more in-depth on this topic, should you want to learn more. I don’t know about you, but creating copious amounts of synthetic credit risk is always a worry. Oh well; what could go wrong?
