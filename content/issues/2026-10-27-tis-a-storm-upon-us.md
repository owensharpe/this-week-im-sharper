## CoreWeave In Distress

We’re beginning to see the iceberg slowly capsize. CoreWeave, the infamous high-yield neocloud, has issued a ton of debt (~$25B) since its March 2025 IPO. Its CEO, Michael Intrator, tried to placate investor sentiment today, but was [met with tumult](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/coreweave-s-brazen-dream-crashes-into-reality-as-yields-top-15?utm_medium=email&utm_source=author_alert&utm_term=261014&utm_campaign=author_23730688):

> CoreWeave shares plunged as much as 17% on Wednesday, the biggest drop since February. Its debt due in 2032, which investors bought at par four months ago, traded at less than 78 cents on the dollar…

It’s fallen further since this article, down roughly 25% this week. My concern is that this company’s business model is not well equipped to sustain any decline in compute demand. Its debt bill is already so large.

> Credit-default swaps on the company are pricing in a more than 50% chance of default within the next five years.

Turning to credit and credit derivatives, there will be more fear in these instruments since they’re closely tied to concerns about CoreWeave, whereas equity sits as a laggard. We’ll see if this comes back down anytime soon. Within the last month or so, those swaps have nearly doubled in spread.

> CoreWeave has drawn outsized attention for being an early entrant in the business that has been heavily reliant on the high-yield debt markets to finance its buildout. Worries have seeped into the bonds backing data centers tied to CoreWeave, with at least seven such securities trading lower this week. …

Now, what would be a baller move by the CoreWeave board would be to issue even more debt to show that it has absolutely no concern over how the debt and equity are trading. I think it’s important to note though that [CoreWeave founders have sold about $3.3B in debt](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/coreweave-founders-shield-fortunes-3-202255611.html?guccounter=1&guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cucmVkZGl0LmNvbS8&guce_referrer_sig=AQAAAJdEE8iAQEOqXzODhuCeN3Tmi-49DNz33u8rCeruHk3-FHWAHjTrlOOiD1tEquZQx59AjraaPlS5-io9YrCTUya-_IVtcn8end5fFkGocX9rD1BDygIh16G5ZqWRogUT9b3SI1JAIK_GBcyOvQwezyGaFF4359WL1hdkQ6g0FBmW) since the IPO in March of 2025.

> That investment-grade dream is crashing into reality, according to D.A. Davidson analyst Gil Luria, who has previously referred to CoreWeave as “the subprime tranche” of AI. Its bonds now yield 15% — a level that is practically unheard of in the primary market for new debt.
>
> CoreWeave Chief Executive Officer Michael Intrator, in an internal email seen by Bloomberg, said the company’s financial position “more than supports” its debt obligations.
>
> “When you are building a new type of technology company there will be periods of time when the market misunderstands and acts irrationally,” Intrator wrote in the email. “We are confident in our business model, our customers, the extraordinary quality of our execution, and the relentless discipline around the financial engine we have built.” …

So why have so many shares been sold? Maybe it’s a run-of-the-mill procedure, but how will that strike confidence into your shareholders and debtholders when they are acting so “irrationally,” apparently?

> CoreWeave has made no secret of its goal to secure a higher credit rating. It said last month in a statement that it has an “objective of migrating its enterprise credit profile toward investment grade.”
>
> CoreWeave’s bonds were among the worst performers across the junk-debt market on Wednesday, according to Trace pricing data. Two portions of its unsecured debt issued this year, due in 2031 and 2032, now trade below 80 cents, which is widely considered the threshold for when a borrower is financially distressed.
>
> “Still, given that its customer contracts are shorter than its lease commitments to Galaxy Digital,” [S&P analyst] added, “it could be challenging for CoreWeave to honor these lease obligations in an environment where the demand for AI services is lower than expected, and the industry is facing excess infrastructure supply relative to demand.”

It’ll have to speed up its backlog burn rate to get anywhere close to an investment-grade rating. CoreWeave carries so much debt that any hint of an interest rate hike exacerbates the risk of its interest burden. I am very concerned about the company’s long-term growth. Jensen Huang doesn’t seem to be, though, but of course he isn’t, since Nvidia’s stake in CoreWeave is 11%.

Also, CoreWeave’s CEO is hilarious for trying to perform damage control. [Some of these quotes are awesome](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/coreweave-s-ceo-blames-rout-on-market-acting-irrationally):

> [CoreWeave Inc. Chief Executive Officer Mike Intrator] said the market is acting “irrationally” and that he remains confident in the AI cloud company’s business model.
>
> CoreWeave’s deal backlog is large enough to more than support its debt obligations, Intrator said in an internal memo reviewed by Bloomberg News. …
>
> “When you are building a new type of technology company, there will be periods of time when the market misunderstands and acts irrationally,” Intrator wrote to staff. “We are confident in our business model, our customers, the extraordinary quality of our execution, and the relentless discipline around the financial engine we have built.”
>
> Intrator said “factors like the weakness and delays surrounding the attempted IPOs and debt financings” of several CoreWeave rivals “are bleeding into the narrative around CoreWeave.”
>
> Intrator signaled that the company will further address the market reaction during its next earnings call. “I, for one, am looking forward to having the opportunity to put the facts in front of the market,” he said.

So confident, Mike. I think he’s wrong; I think it’s more that CoreWeave is a very concerning company with a confusing outlook tied directly to the infrastructure buildout, which is intrinsically sketchy (hello, 2001). That is the narrative that is bleeding into other areas of the market. CoreWeave is tied to companies it has partnerships with that are showing they may not be able to finance future endeavors, which in turn means that CoreWeave will not be able to finance future endeavors.

## Broadcom Plays Banker

Speaking of companies that can’t finance future endeavors on their own, [Broadcom has decided to just do it for them](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/broadcom-arranges-63-billion-deal-to-finance-anthropic-s-chips):

> Chipmaker Broadcom Inc. has arranged a $63 billion deal with Bank of America Corp., Blackstone Inc., Citigroup Inc. and Morgan Stanley to finance the purchase of chips for AI model developer Anthropic PBC.
>
> The deal is being done through a new special purpose vehicle with committed financing from the financial firms to buy Broadcom chips and lease them to Anthropic, the firms said in a joint statement on Monday. Broadcom is backstopping a portion of the financing with what’s known as residual value support.
>
> Bloomberg had reported earlier this month that Broadcom’s Wall Street syndicate had begun gathering roughly $60 billion of fresh AI chip financing to benefit Anthropic and other companies — and that banks including Bank of America, Citigroup and Morgan Stanley were involved.

The structure goes like this: a newly formed special purpose vehicle buys Broadcom’s AI accelerators and networking gear, then leases all of it to Anthropic, and Anthropic’s lease payments pay down the debt. These deliveries start in 2027.

Bank of America, Citi, and Morgan Stanley are arranging roughly $42B of senior secured debt, and Blackstone is putting in $9B of second-lien debt. [We also saw from the FT that](https://www.ft.com/content/cb4034e1-475f-40b9-a5f9-256e91aacb89?syn-25a6b1a6=1)

> Anthropic is offering Blackstone a high yield of more than 10 per cent to finance its latest chip deal, highlighting the start-up’s willingness to stomach steep borrowing costs to finance expensive AI infrastructure.
>
> It is expected to be the largest chip financing to date and a crucial test of investor appetite in AI debt at a time when lenders are increasingly nervous about tech companies’ heavy capital spending and borrowing.
>
> Blackstone funds would invest $9bn in the debt backed by Anthropic’s five-year lease payments on the chips, accounting for about half of the $18bn junior loans, according to people familiar with the matter.

And the deal almost didn’t happen at all. [According to the WSJ](https://www.wsj.com/finance/broadcom-ceo-intervened-to-seal-63-billion-anthropic-debt-deal-c807754f), lenders got cold feet until Broadcom CEO Hock Tan personally stepped in to get it over the line. When the CEO of the vendor has to call the banks himself, that tells you something about how the banks felt.

Now the [banks want to get it off their books](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-27/banks-eye-21-billion-of-bonds-for-broadcom-chip-financing-deal):

> Banks are planning to sell as much as about $21 billion of high-grade bonds as soon as early next year as part of a $63 billion funding package that Broadcom Inc. has pulled together to help finance chips for AI developer Anthropic PBC. …
>
> The banks are lending about $42 billion of the amount, as much as half of which is expected to be refinanced in the US high-grade corporate bond market in the first quarter of 2027, they said, asking not to be identified because they’re not authorized to speak publicly. …
>
> The $42 billion portion of the debt is senior secured…
>
> The bonds that are ultimately sold will probably mature in around five years, and principal on the debt will likely be repaid over the life of the notes. The final size of the bond issuance, and the timing, will depend on market conditions, said the person.

The thing that makes the senior piece “high-grade” is Broadcom. It’s providing residual value on the senior portion, which is basically a promise that if the chips are worth less than the debt when it’s all said and done, Broadcom covers the gap. So the investment-grade buyers are mostly underwriting Broadcom, with an AI lease stapled to it. Anthropic’s actual credit lives in that Blackstone tranche.

Two things bug me here. The first of which is this notion of residual value. What the hell does a 2027 AI accelerator look like in 2032? How much will that’ve depreciated by then? Chips go obsolete very fast; every new generation makes the last one look slow. Broadcom is going to be left holding the bag of excrement if that number comes in poor. Secondly, Broadcom already backstopped up to $29B on a $35B deal with Apollo and Blackstone for Anthropic earlier this year. Moody’s still calls Broadcom one of the strongest large tech companies out there, but it also flagged that the risk goes up if Broadcom keeps doing these in a short period of time. Two in one year is starting to look like a habit.

I said “hello, 2001” in the CoreWeave section, but this one is even more on the nose. Back then, Lucent and Nortel lent their customers billions so those customers could buy Lucent and Nortel equipment. It made revenue look fantastic until the customers couldn’t pay. Broadcom is a much stronger company than either of those was, and Anthropic is not a frivolous telecom startup, but vendor financing is vendor financing.

## How Do You Value Anthropic?

Anthropic [filed its S-1](https://www.ft.com/content/a6c43402-719e-4295-ae5f-541edb0aeb29?syn-25a6b1a6=1) on Monday, and it’s aiming to be public before the end of November. The thing is 335 pages and roughly 240,000 words, which is longer than most of the books I was assigned in high school. It’s shaping up to be the largest IPO ever. Unsurprisingly, the growth numbers are absurd. First-half revenue was $16.3B, about 15 times the first half of last year, and in Q2 revenue was growing at something like 1,368% year over year (WTF??). Then it told investors Q3 came in at [$16.8B](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cqzjx448vxkgo), up 46% from Q2.

There was this great bit from John Foley (Head of Lex) on this podcast called [Unhedged](https://www.ft.com/content/d4771d0e-11ad-4ddb-b6fc-02dc3cd5534d?syn-25a6b1a6=1):

> John Foley: Well, if it grows, I just, back of napkin was like, what if it does grow at this rate for the next five years? And I worked out that it would make something like $584tn of revenue by 2031 (Robert laughs), which is not possible, I don’t think…
>
> John Foley: That’s what we do — cigarette packets. And the one thing that is really useful, which is what management thinks it’s going to make in revenue in future years, they don’t put in the filing. They will talk about it on their roadshow…
>
> John Foley: Well, that is a great question, because what Anthropic is gonna make in revenue and profit, which is what you care about if you’re a shareholder, depends on how quickly people adopt their AI products, how good those products are, what their rivals do, whether there’s a price war, how much they pay for their data centres. Like the variables are innumerable, and every one of them changes because the company’s growing so quickly, it changes the valuation dramatically. If you tweak a knob just a tiny bit in one direction, the valuation changes considerably. So take your pick…I think what’s really gonna happen is they will just pick a number, which is the number they have in mind, which is probably about $1.5tn and everyone will scramble to justify it.

They also discuss that everyone works backwards to justify it, and the justification mostly ends up being “it feels as important as SpaceX or Meta,” which trade around $2T and $1.8T.

Then there’s the governance side of this, which [Lex has a whole piece on](https://www.ft.com/content/0b7db9a1-8279-4025-8c2a-44a1010828f4?syn-25a6b1a6=1):

> The AI lab behind the Claude chatbot, which filed for an initial public offering on Monday, is as K-shaped as it gets. Regular shareholders will have almost no power because the company’s seven founders wield 50.1 per cent on nearly every topic. A separate trust tasked with guarding humankind’s interests elects the majority of the board, but investors have no sway over that either.
>
> Anthropic’s IPO adds an extra flourish to the theme. Normally, even a shareholder with few or no votes can, as a last resort, sue the company for acting against their interests. Some small investors filed such a suit against Meta on Tuesday. But the Claude maker is a “public benefit corporation” with a special mission — to safely build AI. That gives it a shield to do things shareholders may not like. A recent court case involving student loan company MPower Financing confirmed that shareholders in a PBC do not automatically come first.
>
> Taking investors’ money but depriving them of power is undemocratic, but is it bad? There’s a body of evidence suggesting such companies, notably those led by visionary founders, eventually trade at a discount to similar groups with more conventional structures. In a sign that votes may have at least some financial value, Google owner Alphabet’s non-voting “C” shares trade at a tiny discount to its more empowered “A” shares.

Anthropic isn’t alone in this, either. Meta wants to fund its AI spending by issuing new shares with no votes attached, and the [FT reports a $95B offering with Nvidia, AMD, and Broadcom lined up as cornerstone investors](https://www.ft.com/content/94e5a0ae-874d-447a-bbfc-cd465a1a7555?syn-25a6b1a6=1). So the chipmakers are buying stock in their customer so the customer can buy more of their chips. Same movie as the Broadcom section, but call it a different theater.

I keep falling back on this juxtaposition as a self-deemed “credit guy”: the same company is paying Blackstone a double-digit yield on junior chip debt while asking equity investors for something like $1.5T to $2T with essentially no vote. The lenders are getting paid like this is risky. The stockholders are being asked to pay like it isn’t, and they get no say if it turns out it was. Every new deal is hurting my brain.

## Copper Has A Hangover

[Oh no](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-18/traders-grow-wary-of-copper-bull-market-with-no-sign-of-tariffs):

> Until the past few weeks, copper futures in New York had traded at persistent premiums to the global benchmark set on the London Metal Exchange, and that’s enabled traders to make unprecedented profits shipping copper to the US before any levies come in. In turn, inventories outside the US have plunged, with ongoing US imports fueling a feedback loop that helped drive LME prices to a record near $15,000 a metric ton last month.
>
> But with the midterm elections approaching, investors across markets are questioning whether a strong showing for the Democrats will disrupt Trump’s trade agenda — or whether it might even prompt him to double down on tariffs. And for copper bulls, the consequences could be profound if Trump is found to be unwilling or unable to push ahead with the levies.
>
> Analysts at Natixis SA have warned that the tariff trade has created a bubble in the market for one of the world’s most important industrial commodities, and it could soon burst if tariffs are ruled out. Macquarie Group says prices could crash toward $10,000 a ton in that scenario, or they could rally as far as $18,000 if tariffs are announced with a lag that would allow traders to ship even more metal to the US. Three-month futures traded little changed at $14,389 a ton on the LME at 12:28 p.m. in London.
>
> As the midterms approach, there are signs that traders are increasingly willing to bet against tariffs, with their wagers centered on the spread between copper futures on New York’s Comex and the LME. The two markets used to move in near-lockstep, but Comex prices — which would be inclusive of duties if Trump introduces them — have traded at huge premiums over the LME since Trump first formally proposed tariffs on copper in February 2025.

Some quick background information: the US slapped a 50% tariff on semi-finished copper products (pipe, wire, rod, that kind of stuff) back in August 2025 but left refined copper alone, with a phased-in tariff on refined metal floated for 2027. Traders spent months shipping copper into the US to get ahead of it, which is why Comex prices traded at a big premium to London for so long. That premium has mostly evaporated (boohoo), and [at points this fall it’s gone negative](https://www.mining.com/web/column-bruised-funds-retreat-from-copper-after-tariff-turbulence). Meanwhile, there’s a massive pile of copper sitting in US warehouses that only really makes sense if the tariff actually happens. If it doesn’t, that metal is stranded in the wrong place, and the obvious move is to ship it back out.

The other leg of the rally is the AI and electrification story, which was the main long-term play in my mind. Data centers and power grids need a lot of copper; some have made trades exactly on this pitch, but others are talking about a weaker demand outlook. So you have a bull market leaning on two things: a policy decision that keeps not happening, and the same AI buildout that’s wobbling in the credit markets. I wouldn’t call that an extraordinarily strong foundation. If either one cracks, I’d expect copper to give back a decent chunk of this run.

## FX Vol Is Sticking Around Baby

[Quick one to round things out](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-27/barclays-sees-fx-volatility-staying-elevated-as-dollar-rises):

> Barclays Plc strategists expect volatility in Group-of-10 currencies to rise and remain elevated as the dollar strengthens and interest-rate swings increase.
>
> Realized volatility has yet to catch up with the higher levels priced into currency options, though Barclays expects that gap to narrow.
>
> Even if the dollar’s rally pauses because investors are already heavily positioned for further gains, “we would expect the probability of an increase in FX vol to drop only in the very near term,” Barclays strategists Marek Raczko and Leonardo Crimella wrote in a Tuesday note.
>
> Higher currency volatility creates an “unfavorable environment” for carry trades, which seek to profit from differences in interest rates. The strategies have begun to underperform after delivering strong returns since the US announced sweeping tariffs in April 2025. Returns relative to volatility have fallen for most carry currencies since the end of August, the strategists said.
>
> Barclays expects any increase in currency volatility to persist and cautioned against betting on a rebound in carry currencies following sharp selloffs. “We do not see value in chasing global carry trade,” the strategists said, warning that emerging-market currencies are likely to reprice.

That last part is the thing I’d pay attention to. A carry trade is when you borrow in a low-yielding currency and park the money in a higher-yielding one, and it works great right up until volatility spikes and everyone tries to get out at once. The Yen in the summer of 2024 is a good example of this situation; see this [Morgan Stanley research paper](https://www.morganstanley.com/insights/articles/japanese-yen-weakens-after-us-intervention-2026) for some background on it (not sponsored, btw). With the Fed and ECB meeting this week, a hot inflation backdrop, and France’s bond market doing its thing, there are plenty of ways to get that spike. A stronger dollar also makes life harder for anyone around the world with dollar-denominated debt, which is just one more source of pressure on top of everything else in this issue.
