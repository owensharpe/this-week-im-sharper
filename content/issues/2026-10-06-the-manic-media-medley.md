## Goldman Wants More CLO Managers

[Here’s the meat and potatoes](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/goldman-s-bid-for-clo-manager-highlights-wall-street-s-fee-hunt?utm_medium=email&utm_source=author_alert&utm_term=261005&utm_campaign=author_22331638):

> Larger asset managers including Goldman Sachs Group Inc. have been looking to snap up CLO management businesses as they hunt for lucrative fee streams in the $1 trillion US market.
>
> The once-torrid growth in collateralized loan obligations has been slowing and profit margins have thinned, turning the existing stable of CLO firms into juicy takeover targets for larger rivals…
>
> But profit margins on the riskiest portions of CLOs, known as equity, have been driven down to vanishingly small levels. As a result, larger firms now have an advantage. Big CLO firms nowadays frequently raise their own in-house “captive equity” funds, bypassing the onerous step of persuading outside investors to back each new deal.

The idea here is that Goldman has been pushing to grow its asset management business. To my knowledge, I’ve read that David Solomon (Goldman Sachs CEO, or maybe known as “The DJ D-Sol” by some, although he doesn’t get to DJ anymore, seemingly) has made the future of Goldman’s business a fee-based A&WM model. Now, how can you grow your asset management business? Well, you can grow it by pooling investments into funds across a broad range of asset classes, particularly the ones that are in high demand.

The new hot and modern ones are the private spaces (exempli gratia, private equity, private credit, real estate), but CLOs are also an interesting space right now because corporate loans are a hot topic (for good and bad reasons), and there’s also a chance for larger managers to come in and pick off smaller ones at great values. This is (probably?) what Goldman is seeing right now, hence why [they’ve been interested in acquiring Palmer Square](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/goldman-in-talks-to-buy-37-billion-credit-firm-palmer-square).

## Quant Funds Eat Well From Fixed Income

[Extracting those good parts](https://www.ft.com/content/61e4b54a-53f7-43a9-a5dc-d5a6359ba4b0?syn-25a6b1a6=1)…

> Hedge funds relying on quantitative trading strategies have scored big profits from a rout in global government bonds that has pushed US and European borrowing costs to multi-decade highs…
>
> Yields on 10-year US Treasuries have surged from about 4 per cent at the end of February to more than 5.2 per cent, while French, UK and Italian bonds have also sold off sharply…
>
> Among funds to benefit is Connecticut-based Graham Capital, whose Tactical Trend fund is up more than 31 per cent so far this year, including a 3.3 per cent gain last month, according to two people familiar with the matter.
>

>
> Driving the surge in global bond yields and expectations of interest rate rises this year have been soaring crude prices, with benchmark Brent crude up about 40 per cent since the war began in February.

This one’s pretty straightforward. Quant funds in particular have performed very well against the bond sell-off this year. If you’d gone ahead and bet against Treasuries since the beginning of the Iran War, you’d be sitting on a lot of unrealised gains. I think the point of this section is just to show that quant funds have eaten on the macro volatility this year, as the war (and particularly the administration) have caused quite the deviation.

## Soft Defaults

Back to the topic of private credit, there’s been some concern about how [rating agencies are going about tracking private credit defaults](https://www.ft.com/content/04c5abf0-d7c4-47ca-9391-4a154c14e178?desktop=true&segmentId=d8d3e364-5197-20eb-17cf-2437841d178a&syn-25a6b1a6=1#myft:notification:instant-email:content):

> Fitch said it recorded 14 private credit default events in August, the highest monthly tally in the past year and up from three in July…
>
> Fitch monitors two portfolios of private debt. One consists of middle-market loans originated by private credit managers and placed with insurance company clients, who require ratings for regulatory reasons. Another consists of loans that have been bundled into tradeable collateralised debt obligations, which in turn disclose the performance of individual constituents. Together, the two portfolios represent more than 1,650 borrowers…
>
> A bilateral relationship between lender and borrower, the story goes, increases recoveries when companies run into trouble by reducing the chance of a destructive fight among multiple creditors ending in an inefficient liquidation…
>
> Fitch, however, includes “soft” defaults, such as “payment in kind” equity-for-interest swaps and negotiated extensions, in its private credit index. Lyle Margolis of Fitch told Unhedged that “the bottom line is pressure building in these portfolios and that is manifesting as higher defaults . . . the default rate is dominated by soft defaults, but it’s still a sign — if everything was fine, you wouldn’t be seeing this”. The rate is rising for three reasons, Margolis says: a wave of loans reaching maturity, higher interest rates and a slowing market for middle-market M&A. He notes that many private credit borrowers use five to seven turns of leverage, all of which often carries floating rates. And in the past, a company with a difficult capital structure could sell itself without much trouble; it’s harder now…
>
> The loans Fitch tracks may not be perfectly representative of the $2tn private credit industry.

Okay, a bit of a longer pull, but all that context is important. With rates having risen so much, debt-ridden companies in bilateral relationships with private credit funds are turning towards soft defaults as a means to make sure they don’t run into actual defaults or bankruptcies in the future. Fitch would still seemingly classify this as a default, though, even if it's not a regular type of default.

My prediction would be that we only continue to see these soft defaults eat into the majority of overall defaults within the private credit space, unless rates substantially go down. If rates keep going up, then maybe we’ll start to see more actual defaults.

## Private Credit Sees Some Light

Despite the bad private credit default news, [there was some rosy private credit redemption request news](https://www.ft.com/content/f342efa7-96b0-4bb2-aa19-fae740d8c286?desktop=true&segmentId=d8d3e364-5197-20eb-17cf-2437841d178a&syn-25a6b1a6=1#myft:notification:instant-email:content):

> The turbulence that has rocked private credit funds for the past year showed signs of easing in September, with the flood of redemption requests from retail investors slowing and performance improving…
>
> The funds met on average less than half of investor redemption requests and reported muted demand for new investment, restricting earnings growth at some of the world’s largest asset managers…
>
> Performance across the four funds has improved in recent months after a rocky start to the year, though private credit has badly underperformed the overall debt market this year, according to data from PitchBook…
>
> Investors seeking to exit have fewer shares remaining in the funds after successive quarters of withdrawals. Several funds noted that redemptions were driven by investors who had already taken out money in previous quarters.

This is a good thing overall for private credit. One can generalize that redemption requests correlate with investor sentiment. If a fund has a high redemption request figure and a high rate of change over the last few months, one can assume investors who’ve provided the fund with capital are more fearful of the market environment. If a fund has a net inflow of capital, then maybe the investors are quite confident about the fund and its ability to generate returns.

You’d hope the funds continue this trend rather than have a one-off, though. I believe most of the main funds are still receiving net outflows, so we want to see some progression of negative convexity if the redemption requests are decreasing.

## Mortgage Rates Are Not Bad, They're Worse

I remember a time earlier this year when people were complaining about mortgage rates being too high, around 6%. Now, I think some people wished they [could’ve locked in a rate](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/us-mortgage-rates-surge-above-7-to-a-more-than-two-year-high?srnd=homepage-americas) around that time (depending on the length of their mortgage, but alas):

> US mortgage rates rose to the highest level in more than two years, further weighing on a housing market already suppressed by elevated prices and sluggish sales.
>
> The contract rate on a 30-year fixed mortgage rose 15 basis points to 7.12% in the week ended Sept. 18, according to Mortgage Bankers Association data released Wednesday. That was the highest since May 2024. The five-year adjustable-rate mortgage, however, fell 13 basis points to 6.1%.
>
> Rates have been trending up since February, when the start of the Iran war drove energy prices up and rekindled inflation concerns. The Federal Reserve last week raised its benchmark interest rate for the first time since 2023 in an effort to contain price pressures…
>
> Borrowing costs could remain elevated. Mortgage rates closely track the 10-year US Treasury yield, which is hovering near its highest level in almost two decades. Nationwide expects mortgage rates to remain around 7% at least through the end of this year.

Could you imagine being someone who locked in mortgage rates around the end of 2020 or the beginning of 2021? Wow, wouldn’t that have been lovely? Now, mortgage rates really depend on inflation right now, since mortgage rates track the 10-year and the longer-duration treasuries really move off inflation and inflation has remained elevated since the Iran War began, so until the war fixes itself, I don’t see rates going down anytime soon.

However, let’s say in like a year, if the war is over and inflation is approaching 2% (a BIG if), then you’d probably be pissed if you locked in a 7% mortgage rate. Luckily, this isn’t my game to play yet.

## Blackstone Proves You Can Do CDOs of Anything

What a shock; We now have [hybrid CLOs that are mixing private and public credit into one investment vehicle](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/blackstone-works-on-hybrid-clo-blending-private-credit-and-loans)! Isn’t that awesome:

> Blackstone Inc. is developing a CLO that mixes broadly syndicated loans with private credit, according to people familiar with the matter, pairing the higher yields of direct lending with the depth of the leveraged loan market…
>
> Typically, collateralized loan obligations bundle mainly leveraged loans, alongside a small allocation of high-yield bonds, into securities offering varying levels of risk and return. As direct lending has swollen in size, managers in the US have priced over 300 private credit CLOs, worth around $161 billion. Europe, however, has only seen five of such vehicles, according to data compiled by Bloomberg.
>
> The hybrid model allows some European managers, who may lack the scale and diversification of their US counterparts, to build out their portfolios with readily accessible syndicated loans, then add higher-spread private loans as they source them…
>
> The hybrid structure offers a more diversified portfolio of credits with a lower average rating compared with private credit CLOs, the report added…
>
> As larger private credit borrowers in the portfolio explore refinancing options in the broadly syndicated loan market, the CLO manager with access to sensitive information about those companies may face restrictions on their involvement in the refinancing, potentially limiting their options.
>
> Some asset managers restrict the flow of information between their private credit analysts and those covering broadly syndicated loans. That separation can provide greater speed and clarity when operating in either market independently, but can complicate the management of a hybrid CLO spanning both.

This is a really cool article and a great job by Amedeo Goria. I think what he highlights regarding the information of private companies spilling over into the hands of public analysts is very interesting and indeed a major concern.

In my view, I also don’t like that these types of CLOs could then be offered to institutional or other investors, seemingly providing another vehicle that has private assets. Someone could invest in a CLO and lock up their capital when the CLO has mostly public loans, but then maybe they wouldn’t realize the manager has moved private loans in to replace some of the public ones. I just never love the idea of mixing public and private under the same umbrella.

## Oracle's Stuck In The Mud

[Are we surprised](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/cracks-in-oracles-ai-data-center-build-out-appear-in-massive-new-mexico-project-effb51c2?mod=djemTECH):

> Oracle set out last year to lease a massive AI data-center campus being built in New Mexico’s Doña Ana County, part of a blitz of deals to create computing power for OpenAI.
>
> The company made aggressive financial commitments for the facility in contracts that allow it little wiggle room. Now, “Project Jupiter” is mired in challenges over power supply and permitting and is fast becoming a prime example of the risks in the tech giant’s sprawling and expensive artificial-intelligence gambit.
>
> Oracle signed a lease for the New Mexico facility with so-called hell-or-high-water terms, meaning the deal can’t be terminated and Oracle is obligated to make rent payments regardless of whether it has secured the power to operate the data centers, according to people with knowledge of the deal…
>
> Earlier this week, Oracle delivered a force majeure notice to Stack Infrastructure—the site’s developer, which is owned by investment firm Blue Owl Capital funds—that it would push back the date at which it will pay full rent. The lease gives Oracle the right to do that for up to three years…
>
> Pulling the force majeure lever buys Oracle some time but doesn’t reduce its financial exposure to the data center. Oracle can defer payments until the power comes on, but that triggers an automatic extension of the duration of its lease, meaning that the ultimate amount it pays to Stack wouldn’t decline…
>
> At least one bank that arranged New Mexico loans sold off a portion of the debt for a price below 90 cents on the dollar…
>
> The sales of the New Mexico construction loans imply a paper loss of at least $1.8 billion for original lenders…
>
> S&P Global Ratings downgraded Oracle’s credit rating to one notch above “junk” territory in July, saying that its “rapidly expanding AI infrastructure business is increasing its overall credit risk.” Its capital spending on AI equipment and property has exceeded its cash flows from operating its business for several quarters.

Do I think this is concerning? Yes, of course. Do I think the hype from this about Oracle immediately crumbling became overblown? Also yes. This article popped up around the same time as when Treasuries started another tremendous climb.

This article caused people to go observe ORCL yields/CDS and get very scared. It’s important to realize that a good chunk of that yield increase came directly from Treasuries, while the ORCL spreads stayed relatively stable. Articles like these are the ones that always spark investor fear, though.
